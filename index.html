Part 01

Procurement in a building firm

You know the theory. The thing worth locking in is how it actually lands on a trades job — where the money leaks, and what "good" looks like when your suppliers are merchants, plant-hire yards and subcontractors rather than a tidy corporate catalogue.

The whole job, boiled down, is turning a need on site into a paid-for delivery or a completed bit of work — the right stuff, right price, right place, right time, from someone reliable, with a paper trail finance and the taxman can trust. The recognised UK framework for this is the CIPS procurement cycle, and its north star is the Five Rights:

Right 1

Right quality

Fit for the job — the correct grade of timber, the right spec of aggregate, a competent subbie.

Right 2

Right quantity

Enough to keep the job moving, not a yard full of over-ordered stock that walks or warps.

Right 3

Right place

On the right site, where the gang can reach it — not the wrong depot 40 miles away.

Right 4

Right time

There when the trade needs it. Early ties up cash and space; late stops the job.

Right 5

Right price

What you priced the job at — not a number that quietly crept up between quote and invoice.

The point

They're linked

Miss one and it drags the others down — a cheap price that turns up late and wrong isn't a saving.

CIPS formalises the cycle into 13 stages, but for day-to-day it splits neatly into two halves — and knowing which half you're in saves a lot of head-scratching:

Strategic — Source-to-Contract

The thinking half: deciding what you need, finding and vetting suppliers, getting quotes, negotiating, agreeing terms. Done per category or per big package — e.g. setting up a groundworks subbie, or a year's timber deal with a merchant.

Operational — Procure-to-Pay (P2P)

The doing half: the daily loop of requisition → order → delivery to site → invoice → pay. High volume, lots of small orders. This is where you'll spend most days — and where small errors quietly stack up.

Go deeper — category management, the Kraljic Matrix & spend analysis

Once the basics are running, bigger procurement teams — in retail, manufacturing, the public sector — think about their suppliers as a portfolio, not a list. Three tools do most of that thinking. None of them need software; they work as well on the back of an envelope for ten suppliers as they do in a spend-analytics platform for ten thousand.

Category management. Instead of treating every purchase as a one-off, group your spend into categories — timber, aggregates, plant hire, groundworks subbies, M&E subbies — and think about each one as its own small strategy: who's in the market, how much leverage you have, what "good" looks like for that category specifically. In a corporate procurement team this is a full-time job per category; in a firm your size, it's a mental model you apply to your top eight to ten categories by spend.

The Kraljic Matrix. Developed for industrial purchasing and still the standard teaching model in procurement (CIPS included), it plots every category on two axes — how much it affects your business, and how risky or hard-to-replace the supply is — into four quadrants, each with a different strategy:

QuadrantWhat it meansTrade exampleStrategy
Routine
low impact, low risk		Lots of suppliers, low value, easy to switchPPE, consumables, small toolsSimplify — cut admin, don't over-manage it
Leverage
high impact, low risk		High spend, but plenty of suppliers compete for itBulk timber, aggregates, standard plant hireCompete hard — this is where quotes and preferred pricing earn their keep
Bottleneck
low impact, high risk		Low value, but very few suppliers or long lead timesA specialist fixing, a single-source materialSecure supply — hold some stock, find a second source before you need one
Strategic
high impact, high risk		High value and hard to replaceYour key groundworks or M&E subcontractor relationshipPartner — invest time in the relationship, plan together, don't shop it around on price alone

The point isn't the labels — it's that a bottleneck subbie and a leverage merchant deserve completely different amounts of your attention, and Kraljic gives you a quick, defensible way to explain why you're spending your time where you are.

Spend analysis & the 80/20 rule. Also called ABC analysis or the Pareto principle: in most firms, roughly 80% of spend runs through around 20% of suppliers. List your suppliers by annual spend, biggest first, and you'll usually find a small "A list" worth serious attention (negotiation, scorecards, relationship time), a middle "B list" worth a preferred-pricing agreement and light monitoring, and a long "C list" of low-value one-offs not worth much process at all. This is exactly what a preferred supplier list (Part 04) is doing informally — spend analysis just makes it a deliberate, evidenced decision instead of a gut feel.

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO). The purchase price is only ever part of the real cost. TCO adds in delivery, handling, waste/returns, rework from poor quality, downtime while you wait for a late delivery, and — for plant or tools — running and disposal costs. It's the formal name for "total cost delivered" in Part 04's quote-comparison table. Manufacturing and retail procurement teams build detailed TCO models for major purchases; for you, the practical version is simply refusing to compare suppliers on headline price alone.

Part 02

Your patch & where the line is

Here's the trap when your manager isn't procurement-trained and the remit's a bit woolly: it's not that you'll do too little — it's that you'll quietly get blamed for things nobody actually gave you the authority to control. Sorting that is genuinely your first move, and it makes you look on-top-of-it, not needy.

You don't need a fancy job description. You need one page you and your manager both sign up to: what's mine, what's not mine, what I escalate.

Who does what — using a RACI (from scratch)

Not met RACI before? It's a dead-simple grid for pinning down who does what on each task, so nothing falls through the cracks and nobody treads on toes. Tasks run down the side, people across the top, and you put one letter in each box. The four letters:

  • R Responsible — the person who actually does the task (you can have more than one).
  • A Accountable — the one person who owns the outcome and carries the can if it goes wrong. Exactly one per row — that's the golden rule.
  • C Consulted — asked for input before it's done (a two-way conversation).
  • I Informed — just told after it's done (one-way, FYI).

Read each row like a sentence. Take "Issue the PO": you do it and own it (A R), and the site team is simply told it's done (I). That single line settles who raises POs — no more grey area.

Two things people trip on: there's only ever one A per task (if two people are accountable, nobody really is), and A and R are often the same person — that's the A R combo you'll see below, totally normal in a small firm.

Reality check for a tiny firm

You'll often hold two or three letters across a single row yourself — Responsible and Accountable for raising POs, and probably the "finance" column too (Part 06). That's fine — the point of the grid here isn't spreading work across a big team you don't have. It's making the manager's boxes explicit, so it's crystal clear what needs their nod versus what's simply yours to get on with.

How to raise it with your manager

"Quick one — can we spend 20 minutes pinning down what I own, what I run past you, and how much I can commit without checking? I'll write it up as a single page so we've both got it to point back to. Just saves any crossed wires down the line."

This works because you're the one bringing order, and it's an easy "yes" for a non-specialist boss. You're not asking them to design procurement — just to confirm the boundaries. Frame it as protecting the firm, because it genuinely does.

Make your 1:1s count

Your regular 1:1 with the site manager is your main steering wheel — so treat it like a standing meeting with an agenda, not just a catch-up. Run the same five beats every time and you'll walk out with decisions instead of vague nods:

  1. Numbers — a quick spend / jobs update (the one-pager from Part 05).
  2. Decisions I need from you — anything over my limit, new suppliers, approvals to unblock.
  3. Watch-outs — jobs running over, late subbies, plant still on hire.
  4. One improvement — the quick win I'm working on right now (Part 10).
  5. Actions — who's doing what, by when — written down.

Don't let 1:1s be the only check-in

A structured 1:1 covers the agreed agenda — it rarely surfaces "actually, I've been struggling with X for weeks and didn't want to make a thing of it." A short, informal, no-agenda check-in every so often — "how's it actually going, anything I should know that hasn't come up?" — with your manager, and separately with the foreman or site team, catches the things a formal meeting structure quietly filters out. This applies well beyond your manager, too: suppliers, subbies, even the reliability of a system like Ariba all drift quietly if nobody ever checks in when things seem fine — see Part 04 for the supplier version of this habit.

Where this connects to governance

Your delegation-of-authority ladder isn't just an office-politics fix — it's the control an auditor or accountant looks for first in a small business, and it's what "declaring an interest" in Part 11 hangs off. Getting it written down here makes everything downstream easier to defend.

Go deeper — stakeholder mapping & influencing without authority

You have no direct authority over the site manager, the subbies, or the owner — you influence all of them without being able to instruct any of them. That's not a weakness of the role; it's the normal condition of procurement everywhere, from a two-person trades firm to a multinational's supply chain team. Two tools, borrowed from change management and project management, make it deliberate rather than instinctive.

The power/interest grid. A simple 2×2: how much power someone has over your work, against how much interest they take in it day to day.

QuadrantTypical personHow to handle them
High power, high interestYour managerManage closely — regular structured 1:1s, no surprises
High power, low interestThe ownerKeep satisfied — short, sharp updates; don't bury them in detail they didn't ask for
Low power, high interestSite foreman, subbiesKeep informed — they feel the effects of your decisions daily; explain the "why," not just the "what"
Low power, low interestAn occasional one-off supplierMonitor lightly — don't over-invest time here

It sounds obvious once written down, but most people never write it down — which is exactly why misjudged effort ("I sent the owner a fifteen-tab spreadsheet") happens so often.

Influencing without authority. A handful of things reliably work when you can't just tell someone what to do:

  • Lead with their problem, not your process. "This stops you getting blamed for a price you didn't agree to" lands better than "please follow the new procedure."
  • Bring data, not opinions. A specific number ("this cost us £340 last month") is far harder to wave away than "I think we're wasting money."
  • Build a track record of small wins. Trust compounds — this is the whole logic behind "spot it, size it, sell it" in Part 10: deliver one small thing well before asking for a bigger change.
  • Make the easy path the right path. A one-click monday form beats a three-paragraph policy email every time — people follow the path of least resistance, so make that path the compliant one.

Part 03

The buying engine

Your daily loop. Run it as a fixed sequence with two checkpoints — because the checkpoints are what keep the firm's money safe and keep you out of the firing line.

The three-way match, trade version

Before finance pays a merchant or subbie invoice, three things line up:

  • The PO — what we agreed to buy and the price.
  • The delivery note / receipt — proof it actually turned up on site (or the work got done).
  • The invoice — what they're charging.

Match? Pay it. Don't match — short delivery, price crept up, half the load's missing, damaged goods sent back? You've caught it before the money's gone. In a trade where merchant prices move and part-loads are normal, this one control saves serious money.

Watch out — signed delivery notes go walkabout

Deliveries get signed for in the mud and the note vanishes into a van door pocket forever. No signed note = no proof it arrived = you're paying blind. Make it a rule: every delivery note comes back to the office (a photo to a shared inbox is fine). It's the single biggest cause of "we paid for materials that never came" in small firms.

Watch out — segregation of duties

In a small firm the same person often requests, orders, receives and approves — that's the classic weak spot. You don't need a big team to fix it: just make sure no single person can both raise a purchase and confirm it was received/paid on their own. Even the manager casting an eye over delivery notes vs invoices breaks the risky loop. Since you're likely wearing the finance hat too, Part 06 has the realistic playbook for this when it's just you and your manager.

Give the site one front door

Decide how site asks you for stuff — one form, one channel, not five WhatsApps and a shout across the yard. Capture the same fields every time: what, which job, how much, by when, deliver where. Boring, but a clean intake is exactly what makes your job-costing reports write themselves later.

Running this in monday.com

The neatest way to build that front door is a monday form — site fills it in on a phone in twenty seconds, and you can make the job code a required field so it can't arrive incomplete. Each submission lands as an item you can then walk through to "Paid."

Raise an order — the trade version

Use this for normal planned spend. The point is not paperwork for its own sake: it confirms the need, cost, authority and job code before the supplier or site has been promised anything.

  1. Capture the request. Record what is needed, job code, quantity/specification, required date, delivery point and requester.
  2. Check the buying route. Use the agreed supplier/price where one exists; otherwise obtain the level of comparison required by the spend authority.
  3. Get the right approval. Secure approval before commitment, and save the approval with the request or PO.
  4. Raise and send the PO. Include price, delivery, terms, job code and any agreed scope. Ask the supplier to confirm receipt and date.
  5. Track delivery. Make sure someone on site knows the order is coming and that the delivery note or service acceptance will be returned.
Done when: the supplier has a clear PO, the job code and approval are recorded, and the receiving evidence has an owner.

Emergency purchase — move fast without losing the trail

A process exception is sometimes necessary; an undocumented exception is where the risk starts. Use this route only where delay would create a genuine safety, programme or loss-prevention problem.

  1. State the emergency. Record the job, what will happen if you wait, the estimated cost and the supplier proposed.
  2. Get written authority. A text or email from the right manager is enough initially; record who approved it and when.
  3. Commit only what is necessary. Tell the supplier the order is an emergency approval and get the best available confirmation of price and delivery.
  4. Raise the PO immediately afterwards. Link it to the emergency approval; do not leave it until the invoice arrives.
  5. Review the exception. If the same emergency recurs, fix the underlying planning, stock or approval problem in Part 09.
Important boundary

An internal PO rule does not cancel a valid contract or justify withholding an undisputed, due payment. Record the exception using the emergency purchase record, query only the disputed amount where appropriate, and seek manager/accountant or legal advice if contract deadlines are in play.

Check an invoice — match, query or pay

  1. Identify the type. Is this goods, plant, a service/valuation, a credit note, or a retention-related payment? Pull the PO/contract and receiving evidence.
  2. Match the basics. Check supplier, job code, quantity or accepted work, price, VAT treatment, retention and payment terms.
  3. Choose the route. If it matches, approve it for the agreed payment run. If it does not, identify whether the difference is price, quantity, quality, tax, duplicate billing or missing evidence.
  4. Query in writing. Send the PO reference, exact variance, evidence and the remedy needed; record the date and owner of the query.
  5. Protect the deadline. Pay any undisputed amount when due and escalate promptly where a construction-contract payment notice, pay-less notice or final-date issue may apply.
Done when: it is approved for payment, or the exact reason for the hold, owner, supplier correspondence and next action are all recorded in the invoice exception log.

Payment notices — the legal clock behind "pay it or dispute it"

This is worth knowing because it can be law, not convention. Many business-to-business construction contracts are covered by the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 (as amended) — usually called the Construction Act. Scope, exclusions and the actual contract matter, so use the contract's dates first and get advice early when money or a formal dispute is involved. Where the Act applies and the contract does not provide an adequate payment mechanism, the Scheme for Construction Contracts may fill the gap.

Go deeper — contract families, warranties & a Lean lens on the buying engine

The main UK construction contract families. You don't need to draft contracts, but recognising which family a job sits under helps you understand the paperwork flowing past you:

  • JCT (Joint Contracts Tribunal) — the most widely used suite in UK building work, with versions scaled to project size (Minor Works, Intermediate, Standard, Design & Build). Traditional, well understood by most small and mid-size contractors.
  • NEC (currently NEC4) — increasingly common, especially on infrastructure and public sector work, built around proactive collaboration and an "early warning" mechanism that requires both sides to flag problems as soon as they're spotted, not after the fact.
  • FIDIC — the international equivalent, mostly relevant if your firm ever works on projects with overseas funding or clients.
  • Bespoke / in-house terms — very common for smaller subcontract packages, often based on one of the above but simplified.

The practical relevance: notice periods, payment mechanics and what counts as a valid instruction can differ between families, so if a dispute is brewing, checking which contract family you're under (and reading that specific document) matters more than general knowledge.

Collateral warranties. A promise from a subcontractor or consultant to someone outside the main contract — typically a funder, a future purchaser, or a tenant — that they've properly carried out their obligations and that party can sue them directly if something goes wrong. You'll mostly encounter these as a paperwork request on larger jobs; know that they exist so a client asking for one doesn't catch you off guard.

Liquidated damages (LDs). A pre-agreed sum written into a contract for late completion, meant to reflect a genuine pre-estimate of loss rather than a penalty. They matter to procurement because they can flow downhill: if your firm is under an LD clause with its client and a late material delivery causes the delay, that cost pressure can end up passed back to the supplier who caused it — which is one more reason lead times and delivery dates in Part 04's quote comparison aren't just nice-to-haves.

Money leak — off-hire discipline

Plant keeps charging until you formally off-hire it and get an off-hire number. A dumper that finished on Tuesday but wasn't off-hired till the following Monday is six days of dead money. A classic: an email that looks like it's from a merchant or subbie asking you to "update our bank details." Never change payment details off an email alone. CIS — Construction Industry Scheme, in full

When the firm pays most subcontractors for construction work, it generally has to operate CIS: verify the subcontractor with HMRC, deduct tax from the labour element of each payment, and pay that over to HMRC on the firm's behalf. It's the mechanism that stops labour-only cash payments slipping outside the tax system.

VAT domestic reverse charge — the one that catches people out

Since March 2021, most standard- or reduced-rated construction services supplied between two VAT-registered, CIS-registered businesses use the domestic reverse charge: the supplier doesn't charge VAT on the invoice — instead, the customer accounts for both the output and input VAT on their own return. It exists to stop a specific fraud (suppliers collecting VAT and vanishing before paying it to HMRC).

Five-question reverse-charge route
  1. Is this a supply of building or construction services, rather than goods-only?
  2. Are the supplier and customer UK VAT registered, and is the payment reported within CIS?
  3. Is the supply standard- or reduced-rated, rather than outside the charge or zero-rated?
  4. Is the supplier genuinely supplying construction services, rather than simply supplying staff as an employment business?
  5. Has the customer given a valid written end-user or intermediary-supplier notification? If yes, normal VAT rules can apply; if not, use HMRC's technical guide before deciding.
SituationReverse charge?
Both parties VAT & CIS registered, standard supply chain (e.g. subbie → your firm)Yes — subbie invoices with no VAT added, states reverse charge applies
Your firm invoicing an end user (e.g. the property owner / developer who won't make an onward construction supply)No — normal VAT rules once the end user has confirmed their status in writing
Materials-only supply with no labour, no installationNo — reverse charge only applies to construction services

A reverse charge invoice must clearly state that the customer is required to account for the VAT — wording like "Reverse charge: customer to pay the VAT to HMRC" — and show the VAT amount or rate that would have applied, without adding it to the total charged. If your firm is ever the "end user" or "intermediary supplier" at the top of a supply chain, tell your suppliers in writing so they invoice you correctly.

Retention — know the mechanics, and the direction it's moving

On subcontract work it's normal to hold back a slice of each payment as security against defects — typically 5%, often halving to 2.5% at practical completion, with the remainder released after the defects period. Track what's held and owed, in both directions, so money doesn't get quietly forgotten. Best practice: agree retention terms before signing, not when you're chasing it back later.

Retention is on its way out — track this one properly

The Commercial Payments Bill, introduced to Parliament in May 2026, would prohibit deducting or withholding retention under a construction contract — alongside a 60-day cap on commercial payment terms and stronger Small Business Commissioner powers, including the ability to investigate poor payers, adjudicate disputes outside court and impose financial penalties. The government has said it will consult further on the timing of the retention prohibition and allow a lead-in period, so this will arrive in stages rather than overnight.

A Bill is not law. Nothing changes on any existing contract unless and until it is enacted and commenced, and the detail can change as it goes through Parliament. Treat this as something to check quarterly, not something to act on yet.

What to actually do about it now

Three things, none of which cost anything. One: make sure your retention schedule is genuinely complete and accurate in both directions — held on your subbies, and held by clients on you (retention schedule template). When a release rule changes, the firms that get their money back quickly are the ones who already knew exactly what was owed. Two: note the date each retention clause was agreed, since any phasing is likely to turn on when a clause was created. Three: put "check retention and payment reform status" on the annual sweep in Part 12 — and actually check it.

Insurance & competence — what to check before work starts

  • Public liability insurance — covers injury/damage to third parties or property; most main contractors and clients expect a minimum level of cover (commonly £2m–£10m depending on the job) — check what your firm's own contracts require.
  • Employer's liability insurance — a legal requirement (under the Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969) for any subbie who has employees of their own, currently with a statutory minimum of £5 million cover. A genuinely self-employed one-person subbie typically doesn't need this, but check their status.
  • CSCS card (Construction Skills Certification Scheme) — the industry-standard proof that an individual on site has the relevant training and health & safety awareness for their role. Most principal contractors and many sites won't let an uncarded worker through the gate.
  • RAMS (Risk Assessment & Method Statement) — a written statement of the hazards on a task and how the subbie will control them, expected for higher-risk work under the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 (CDM 2015) — the health & safety law that sets duties for clients, designers, principal contractors and contractors on construction projects, including small ones. You're not the health & safety officer, but checking that the named safety/site owner has approved the applicable evidence before work starts is a genuinely useful procurement-side control.

Employed or self-employed? The question behind every subbie

This is the single biggest tax risk sitting quietly in a small building firm, and it's routinely misunderstood. The thing to internalise: CIS is a deduction scheme, not a status test. Verifying a subcontractor with HMRC and deducting 20% tells you nothing about whether that person is genuinely self-employed. A UTR only shows someone is registered for self assessment — it does not make them self-employed for the work they're doing for you.

Who carries the cost if it's wrong

If HMRC later decides a "subbie" was in reality an employee, the liability for unpaid PAYE, employee National Insurance and employer's National Insurance falls on the engaging firm — your firm, not the worker — usually with interest and penalties, and often across several years and several workers at once. It is one of the few procurement-adjacent mistakes that can genuinely threaten a small firm's survival. Which is exactly why the onboarding gate in the subbie task guide says this must not become one person's unofficial legal decision.

The long-standing labour-only gang is the classic risk

When what you bought or agreed is wrong — and your rights as the buyer

Materials turn up wrong, short, or faulty; a subbie's work doesn't meet a reasonable standard; a supplier tries to wriggle out of what was agreed. You have real legal footing here, not just the strength of your relationship with them — knowing the shape of it means you can push back calmly and specifically instead of just hoping they'll be reasonable.

A blanket "no returns, no exceptions" line buried in small print is exactly the kind of clause that can fail that test. You're not expected to litigate this yourself — but knowing it exists means you don't have to accept "that's just our policy" as the end of the conversation.

Worked examples — CIS, VAT reverse charge & retention with real numbers

The mechanics in this part read abstractly until you see them run with numbers. Four worked examples, using illustrative figures — swap in your firm's real ones.

CIS deduction. A registered, verified subbie invoices £1,500 for a week's work: £1,000 labour, £500 materials.

  • Deduction applies to the labour element only: 20% of £1,000 = £200.
  • You pay the subbie £1,300 net (£500 materials + £800 labour after deduction).
  • The £200 is remitted to HMRC as part of your monthly CIS return, and the subbie gets a payment and deduction statement showing it.
  • If that same subbie hadn't been verified or registered, the deduction would be 30% of the labour (£300), not 20% — a real incentive to chase paperwork before the first payment, not after.

VAT domestic reverse charge. The same subbie, now VAT-registered, invoices £1,000 + labour-only construction services to your VAT-registered firm.

  • The invoice shows no VAT charged — instead it states the reverse charge applies and shows what the VAT rate/amount would have been (e.g. "VAT reverse charge: 20% — £200 — customer to account for this to HMRC").
  • Your firm records £200 output VAT (as if you'd charged yourself) and £200 input VAT (as if you'd been charged) on the same VAT return.
  • Net cash impact: nil — but both entries must appear, and getting this wrong is one of the most common VAT errors HMRC checks for in construction.

Retention. A £10,000 subcontract package with 5% retention, halving at practical completion.

  • Total retention held across the job: £500.
  • At practical completion, half (£250) is released.
  • The remaining £250 is released once the defects period ends with no outstanding issues.
  • If your firm is also holding retention from a client on the same job, track both directions separately — it's easy to net them off in your head and lose track of which is actually owed to whom.

Statutory late payment interest. A client pays a £5,000 invoice 30 days after the due date.

  • Interest rate: Bank of England base rate (illustrative 4.75%) + 8% = 12.75% per year.
  • Daily rate on £5,000: £5,000 × 12.75% ÷ 365 ≈ £1.75/day → 30 days ≈ £52.35.
  • Plus fixed compensation for the cost of chasing it: £40 for debts under £1,000, £70 for £1,000–£9,999, £100 for £10,000 and over. On a £5,000 debt, that's £70.
  • Total you could legitimately claim: roughly £122 — modest on one invoice, but the principle (and simply mentioning it) is often enough to change a slow payer's behaviour.
Go deeper — supplier segmentation, negotiation frameworks & sustainable sourcing

Supplier scorecards & SRM maturity. Larger organisations run formal Supplier Relationship Management (SRM) programmes, grading relationships from purely transactional up through cooperative to genuine strategic partnership. You can borrow the lightweight version: a simple scorecard for your preferred list — on-time delivery %, quality/defect rate, price competitiveness, and responsiveness when something goes wrong — reviewed quarterly. It turns "I think they're good" into something you can show your manager.

Negotiation frameworks worth knowing.

  • BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) — know what you'll do if this negotiation fails before you start. If you have a genuine second merchant who'll match the price, you're negotiating from strength; if you don't, that's worth fixing before you're in the room.
  • ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement) — the overlap between the most you'd pay and the least they'd accept. Most negotiations that stall have a ZOPA that neither side has found yet, usually because nobody's asked the right question.
  • Anchoring — the first number mentioned pulls the rest of the conversation toward it. Don't be afraid to open with a specific, justified number rather than waiting to react to theirs.

Sustainable & responsible sourcing. ISO 20400 is the international guidance standard for sustainable procurement — it's guidance, not something you get certified against, and it's rarely something a firm your size pursues formally. But its underlying principles (reducing waste, considering whole-life environmental impact, checking suppliers treat their workers fairly) increasingly show up as expectations from bigger main contractors and clients on tenders, and connect directly to the CIPS ethics principles and Modern Slavery Act awareness already covered in Part 12. Knowing the name is useful if a client questionnaire ever asks about it.

Part 06

When you're also finance

In a firm this small there's usually no one to "hand it to finance" — a lot of the time that's you too, with your site manager for backup and maybe one other pair of hands. This sits right on top of the buying flow you already know. When a bill comes in:

The 20 minutes that finds money — statement reconciliation

Once a month, take each main merchant's statement (their list of what you owe them) and check it against your records. It routinely turns up missing invoices, double-charges, and credits they owe you for returns. Suppliers make mistakes in their favour more often than yours — this is how you catch them.

Pay to terms — cash flow is king in construction

  • Batch it. Run payments on a regular day (say weekly), not ad-hoc every time an invoice lands — it's calmer and easier to control.
  • Pay on terms. Not so early you strangle the firm's cash, not so late you burn merchants and subbies. Fair, on-time subbie payment is reputation on site — and it's the spirit of the Construction Act and Prompt Payment Code.
  • Early-payment discounts are only worth taking if the discount beats the value of holding onto the cash.

  • CIS return done (with your accountant)

Your right to be paid on time too — and what happens if you're not

The same coin has another side: if a client pays your firm late, the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 gives UK businesses a statutory right to charge interest — currently set at the Bank of England base rate plus 8% — on the overdue amount, plus a fixed compensation sum (currently £40, £70 or £100 depending on the size of the debt) to cover the cost of chasing it. You don't have to invoke it every time a payment runs a day late, but knowing it exists is real leverage in a difficult conversation, and it's often enough just to mention it.

This is about to get stronger — the Commercial Payments Bill

The Commercial Payments Bill, introduced in May 2026, would cap commercial payment terms at 60 days with strictly limited exemptions, give the Small Business Commissioner powers to investigate poor payers, adjudicate disputes outside court and impose financial penalties, and prohibit construction retentions (Part 04). It is a Bill, not law — but the direction is clear enough to be worth two things now: check your own terms with suppliers would already comply, and keep your late-payment evidence (dates, chasers, promises made) in a form you could actually hand over. Add "check payment reform status" to the annual sweep in Part 12.

Handling supplier data & bank details responsibly

Supplier and subbie records — names, addresses, UTRs, bank details, sometimes right-to-work documents — count as personal data under the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 where they relate to individuals (a sole trader or self-employed subbie, not just a limited company). The practical version for you: only collect what you actually need, keep it somewhere access-controlled rather than in loose email threads, don't forward bank details around casually, and delete or archive records you no longer need once your firm's retention policy allows it. This isn't a separate job — it's mostly the same good records hygiene this whole playbook already asks for.

Spotting & fixing what's broken

You're already in the seat, so forget a 90-day induction — the useful skill now is diagnosis: reading what's actually there, working out where it's leaking, knowing what "good" looks like for a firm this size, and taking that to your manager in a way that gets a yes. Do this well and you go from "the new buyer" to "the person who sorted our buying out."

First, map what's actually happening now

You can't fix a process you can't see — and in a small firm there often isn't a written one; it lives in people's heads and habits. So before you change anything, map the current reality. Quickest way: follow one real order end to end and note each hop exactly as it happens today, not how it's "meant" to.

  • When site needs something, how do they ask — text, call, shout across the yard, a monday item?
  • Who decides it's OK to buy — and is there any spend limit?
  • Who places the order, and does a PO actually exist — or does someone just ring the merchant?
  • On delivery, who signs, and where does the note end up?
  • When the invoice arrives, how's it checked before it's paid?
  • Where's any of it tracked — monday, a spreadsheet, Ariba, or nowhere?

Do that for two or three orders and the gaps jump straight out — that's your diagnosis, grounded in what really happens rather than what people say happens. Sketch it as a simple flow (boxes and arrows on paper is plenty) and you've also got something concrete to show your manager.

How to actually approach it — spot it, size it, sell it

Don't land on your manager's desk with a list of everything that's wrong — that reads as criticism and it's overwhelming. Work it in three moves:

Move 1

Spot it

Quietly observe for a few weeks. Use the target table and the diagnostic above to note gaps. Get the facts — a rough figure on the leak (e.g. "~£X/month in late off-hires").

Move 2

Size it

Rank the gaps by money saved ÷ effort. Pick one or two quick wins first — visible, low-risk, hard to argue with. Build the case in £ and cash flow, the things a builder feels.

Move 3

Sell it

Take one idea at a time, framed as a fix not a criticism. Show the before/after and what you need from them. Deliver the quick win, then propose the next. Trust compounds.

Framing it for your manager

"I've been getting under the skin of how we buy, and there's one quick win I'd start with — [e.g. plant that's sat off-job still on hire]. I reckon it's costing roughly [£X] a month, and the fix is small: [a live on-hire list]. Want me to run with that one, and I'll bring the next when it's bedded in?"

One idea, sized in money, framed as help, with a clear ask. That's how a non-specialist boss says yes — and how you earn the room to fix the bigger stuff later. You've got this.

Your two default moves

When something's unfamiliar and you're not sure what to do, these two cover most of it:

1. Don't let money leave while it's disputed. Pausing a payment is reversible; paying wrongly is much harder to undo. It's completely normal to say "I'm holding this until we've checked it."

2. Put it in writing. A short email confirming what was agreed ("just to confirm — you'll credit the £180 for the damaged sheets") turns a fuzzy phone call into something you can point at later. This habit alone will save you repeatedly.

When you've made the mistake

You will, at some point — wrong quantity, missed a date, paid something twice. Everyone in this job has. The move is always the same: flag it early, say what happened, say what you're doing about it. A mistake surfaced quickly is an admin task; the same mistake found later by someone else is a trust problem. Managers remember how you handled it far more than what it was — and "I've spotted an error, here's my fix" reads as competence, not failure.

Go deeper — supplier risk & what happens if a key subbie goes under

Single-source risk. Relying on one merchant or one subbie for something critical to a job feels efficient right up until they can't deliver — a lorry breaks down, a subbie firm has a bad month, a specialist material is suddenly out of stock. For anything sitting in the "bottleneck" or "strategic" quadrants of the Kraljic Matrix (Part 01's deep dive), it's worth deliberately knowing who your second choice would be, even if you never use them.

Business continuity — a subbie firm going under mid-job. It happens more often in construction than most industries, and it's worth knowing the warning signs and the response before you need them: sudden requests for payment upfront against normal terms, missed deadlines with vague excuses, or key people suddenly unreachable are all early flags. A quick free check of a limited company's filing history on Companies House can surface overdue accounts or other warning signs. If a subbie does go under, your retention holdback (Part 04) becomes a genuinely important protection — it's part of why agreeing it properly in writing matters, not just as routine paperwork.

Mediation. A softer, non-binding alternative to adjudication — a neutral third party helps both sides find a resolution, without imposing one. Cheaper and less adversarial than adjudication, and worth suggesting for a relationship you want to preserve rather than one you're prepared to burn.

Governance, ethics & compliance

This is the section that turns "we trust each other" into something you could actually show an auditor, a new owner, or your own conscience. None of it is complicated — it's mostly the habits already threaded through this playbook, gathered in one place and given their proper names.

The CIPS Code of Conduct — ten principles

CIPS (the Chartered Institute of Procurement & Supply) sets out ten components of ethical procurement that underpin its professional Code of Conduct. You don't need the letters after your name to use them as your own standard:

1 · Transparency, confidentiality & fairness

All suppliers treated fairly at every stage of the process.

2 · Use of power

Never abuse buying power or unfairly pressure a supplier.

3 · Corruption

No tolerance for corruption in any form, direct or indirect.

4 · Declaring an interest

Disclose anything personal that could affect your impartiality.

5 · Social responsibility

Address worker welfare across the supply chain — safety, fair treatment, non-discrimination.

6 · Forced labour

No forced, bonded or involuntary labour anywhere in the chain.

7 · Employment relationship

Workers on legal contracts with clear pay and rights.

8 · Health & safety

Working conditions must be safe and hygienic.

9 · The law

Suppliers operate within their own legal framework.

10 · Wages & hours

Pay and hours meet industry or legal benchmarks.

In a small trades firm, principles 1–4 are the ones you'll live day to day; 5–10 matter most when vetting a new subbie or supplier — a quiet gut-check of "would I be comfortable explaining this relationship to my manager?" covers most of it.

Conflicts of interest — declare, don't hide

A conflict of interest isn't automatically wrong — using your brother-in-law's plant hire yard isn't a scandal. Not declaring it is the problem. The fix is simple and costs nothing: keep a short written log of any personal connection to a supplier, and get your manager to sign off any order to that supplier rather than approving it yourself.

Gifts, hospitality & the Bribery Act 2010

The Bribery Act 2010 is UK law with real teeth: it's an offence to give, offer, or accept a bribe (a financial or other advantage intended to induce improper conduct), and there's also a corporate offence of a company failing to prevent bribery by people acting on its behalf — a defence only exists if the firm had "adequate procedures" in place. Penalties are serious: up to 10 years' imprisonment for individuals and an unlimited fine for the offence itself.

None of this means you can never accept a branded calendar or a Christmas hamper. The test is proportionality and transparency:

"That's really kind, but we keep it simple on gifts while we're placing orders — I'll have to say no, but I appreciate the thought. Be great to catch up over a coffee sometime instead."

Fair treatment of workers in the supply chain

The Modern Slavery Act 2015 requires a formal transparency statement only from larger businesses (turnover above £36 million), so it likely doesn't apply to your firm directly — but its spirit does, and it's exactly what CIPS principles 5–7 above are getting at. A practical version for a small trades firm: ask enough about a new subbie to be satisfied their own workers are paid fairly, on legal contracts, and not visibly exploited. It's rarely a formal check — more a "does something feel off here" instinct, backed by the CIS and insurance paperwork you're gathering anyway.

Go deeper — records management as its own discipline, and the GDPR balancing act

Records management (ISO 15489). Larger organisations run formal "records management" as a discipline in its own right — classifying documents, assigning retention periods systematically, and auditing that the schedule is actually followed. ISO 15489 is the international standard behind this. You don't need the standard itself, but the core idea — write the retention schedule once, then follow it consistently rather than deciding case by case — is exactly what the table above is for.

The GDPR tension. Notice the retention table above pulls in two directions: tax and contract law can require records for years; data protection law requires you to keep personal data no longer than necessary for the documented purpose. The practical resolution is to map what personal data appears in each record, document the lawful reason and retention period, restrict access, then delete or securely dispose of separate personal-data records when the purpose ends. Do not alter evidence held for tax, contract or dispute purposes without checking the applicable requirement.

Version control on plans, not just retention. A management plan that's out of date is worse than no plan — it gives false confidence. Whatever you write (a procurement plan, a retention schedule), date it, note when it was last reviewed, and set a recurring reminder to revisit it rather than letting it quietly go stale.

Raising a concern

If you ever spot something that looks like genuine wrongdoing — not just a mistake, but something that feels deliberately hidden — take it seriously. Whistleblowing protection is conditional: a worker must reasonably believe the disclosure shows a qualifying wrongdoing, that it is in the public interest, and use an appropriate route. In a firm this small, a calm written report to the owner may be suitable unless they are implicated; then use a senior alternative, legal adviser or the relevant prescribed body. Keep to what you have seen and preserve evidence — not gossip or suspicion.

A risk register. A simple table — risk, likelihood, impact, mitigation, owner — used across every industry as the standard way to make risk visible instead of just worried-about. Batching & rhythm — the quiet productivity win

Context-switching is expensive. Where you can, batch similar tasks into fixed slots rather than reacting to everything the moment it lands:

  • Order processing — set times to work through the intake board, rather than reacting to every ping.
  • Payment runs — one batch a week, not ad-hoc (Part 06).
  • Supplier calls — group them; ringing three merchants back-to-back is faster than spread across a day.
  • Reporting — build the monthly pack in one sitting once the data's clean, not piecemeal across the month.
Protect the interrupts that matter

Batching doesn't mean ignoring genuine urgency — a stuck delivery holding up a gang on site still needs an immediate response. The point is separating the truly time-critical from the merely convenient-to-ask-now, so your day has some shape to it.

When you're the only one who knows — cover, holidays and handover

In a firm this size you are a single point of failure, and nobody notices until you're off. A week's holiday, a bad flu, or the day you eventually move on — in each case the firm needs to keep buying, paying and answering suppliers without you. This is not a nice-to-have; it's the same continuity thinking you'd apply to a single-source supplier (Part 11), pointed at yourself. Done properly it also makes you look far more senior than the person who is simply irreplaceable because nothing is written down.

Write it once and keep it current — a single page covering who your key suppliers and contacts are, what's in flight, which deadlines are fixed, where things are filed, and the three things most likely to go wrong. The test worth running once a year

Take a genuine week off and don't be reachable. If the firm coped, your controls work. If it didn't, you've found exactly which gap to fix — and you've found it on a quiet week rather than in an emergency. A function that only works while one person is at their desk isn't a function yet, it's a habit.

Go deeper — the continuous-improvement models this loop is quietly borrowed from

The quarterly cycle above isn't a trades-industry invention — it's a small-scale version of improvement models used across manufacturing, healthcare, retail and IT. Knowing the proper names is useful two ways: it helps you recognise the same idea when a bigger client or a new employer uses different language, and it means you can borrow more of the toolkit once the basics are working.

ModelWhat it actually isHow it maps onto what you're already doing

Do.

  • Write a one-page "state of procurement" annual report for the owner — what's improved, what it's worth, what's next.
  • Draft a category plan for the coming year — which supplier relationships need attention, where the next Kraljic-quadrant risks sit.
  • Look outward: is the firm bidding for the right work, and is the evidence pack good enough to say yes quickly when the right job appears (Part 18)?

Part 17

Mistakes to watch for

The hardest part of this seat isn't the procurement — it's that there's no one senior in the building to tell you whether you're doing it right. This part is the closest thing to that second pair of eyes: how to tell you're on track, the errors people new to procurement make most often, and — because your manager's learning this role too — the patterns worth gently watching for in how spend gets managed above you, with what you could suggest instead.

How do you know you're doing this right?

You won't get a performance review from a procurement specialist, so borrow this instead. None of these are dramatic — that's the point. Good procurement in a small firm is mostly boring, and boring is what you're aiming for.

Signs it's going well
  • You can find any PO, delivery note or invoice from the last six months in under a minute
  • Nobody on site has ordered something "off the books" in months, because the front door is genuinely the easy option
  • Your manager's surprises are good ones (a saving, a rebate) rather than bad ones (a bill nobody expected)
  • When you make a mistake, you're the one who spots it first
  • You can explain why you picked a supplier, not just that you did
Worth a closer look
  • You're regularly paying invoices you haven't actually matched, because there's no time
  • You genuinely don't know what's still on plant hire right now
  • The same question keeps landing on your desk with no clear answer written down anywhere
  • You're avoiding raising something because you're not sure it's your place
  • You can't remember the last time you checked in with a supplier when nothing was wrong

If you recognise more from the second list than the first, that's not a verdict on you — it's a map of where to spend your next few weeks. Pick one, fix it, move to the next. That's the whole job, most weeks.

Your payment rights on public work — worth knowing cold

This is the part almost nobody in a small firm knows, and it is genuinely valuable. Under the Procurement Act, 30-day payment terms are implied into public contracts and cascade down the supply chain — to you as a subcontractor, and to your own subbies below you. The clock runs from receipt of a valid and undisputed invoice, not from whenever somebody gets round to approving it.

  1. It applies even where the contract says otherwise. The 30-day term is implied into public sub-contracts by statute. A main contractor's standard 60-day terms don't override it on a public job.
  2. The authority is expected to check. Contracting authorities publish payment compliance data and carry out spot checks on payments down the chain, precisely to catch this.
  3. It stacks with the Construction Act. Payment notices, pay-less notices and the right to adjudicate (Part 03) all still apply. This is an extra protection, not a replacement.
  4. Say it calmly, in writing. "This is a public sub-contract, so the 30-day payment term under the Procurement Act applies — the invoice was valid and undisputed on [date]." That sentence resolves a surprising number of stalled payments without anything escalating.
Not legal advice

Whether a specific contract sits inside the public regime depends on who the ultimate client is and how the chain is structured. If real money is being withheld, confirm the position with your manager and, where it's material, a solicitor — see the escalation ladder in Part 11.

Should you even bid? A bid / no-bid gate

Tendering costs real time, and the most expensive tenders are the ones you were never going to win. A five-minute gate before anyone starts writing saves days over a year — and gives you a defensible answer when someone asks why you didn't chase a job.

Saying no to a client you want to keep

"Thanks for thinking of us — having looked at it properly, we're not going to bid this one; it's outside what we could staff properly to the programme you need. I'd rather tell you that now than give you a number we'd struggle to stand behind. Do keep us on the list for [the kind of work you do want] — that's squarely our patch."

Always ask for the feedback

Win or lose, ask why. Public bodies must tell unsuccessful bidders how they were assessed; main contractors usually will if you ask politely. Keep a one-line log of every bid, the outcome and the reason (bid log template) — after half a dozen entries a pattern appears, and it's exactly the evidence that turns "we should price more work" into a real conversation with the owner.

Go deeper — frameworks, dynamic markets, and how small firms actually get on lists

Frameworks. Rather than tendering every job, many public bodies appoint a panel of suppliers to a framework for a period of years, then "call off" individual jobs from it — sometimes by rotation, sometimes by mini-competition between framework members. Getting onto a relevant framework is often a bigger prize than any single job, because it turns one bid into several years of invited opportunities. Frameworks are advertised like any other opportunity, so alerts matter.

Dynamic markets. The Procurement Act's replacement for the older dynamic purchasing system. Unlike a framework, a dynamic market stays open — suppliers can apply to join at any point rather than only during an initial window. If you missed a framework, check whether a dynamic market covers the same work.

Lotting. Larger contracts are often divided into lots — by trade, by geography, or by value — specifically so smaller firms can bid for a slice rather than the whole thing. If a contract looks far too big, read the lot structure before dismissing it.

Being a named subcontractor. The lowest-effort route in. Main contractors bidding public work often have to describe their supply chain, and being on their list before they bid means you're carried along with their win without running a tender yourself. That's exactly why the evidence pack above is worth keeping current even in a quiet spell.

Exclusions and the debarment list. The Act sets out mandatory and discretionary grounds on which a supplier can be excluded — serious offences, poor prior performance and similar — plus a central debarment list of suppliers excluded across the public sector. Part of your Central Digital Platform registration is declaring that none apply. It's a straightforward declaration for most firms, but it isn't a box to tick without reading, and it's one more reason the governance habits in Part 12 earn their keep.

A word on portals. You'll accumulate logins — Find a Tender, the Central Digital Platform, one or two main-contractor portals, your assessment body. Keep the account owner, login route and renewal date for each in one list that isn't only in your head. It's a five-minute job that prevents a genuinely stupid failure: missing a deadline because the only person with the password is on holiday (Part 12).

Waste duty of care — the legal bit

Under the waste duty of care, any business that produces waste must take all reasonable steps to keep it safe, hand it only to someone authorised to take it, and document every transfer. Skips, muckaway, strip-out, packaging, offcuts, old boilers — it all counts. This is the one item in this part with genuine enforcement risk, and it's routinely handled badly in small firms because it looks like the skip company's problem. It isn't: the duty stays with the producer.

  1. Check the carrier is registered — every time, before the first load. Waste carriers must be registered with the Environment Agency (or SEPA, NRW, NIEA). The public register is free and takes a minute. An unregistered "man with a van" is how a firm's waste ends up fly-tipped with its own paperwork inside it — and the producer gets the letter.
  2. Get a waste transfer note for every load that leaves site. It records what the waste is, how much, the codes, who handed it over and who took it. Both parties keep a copy.
  3. Describe the waste properly. A vague description is the most common defect. Include the EWC code where you can — the transfer note is the evidence that you handed over what you said you handed over.
  4. Treat hazardous waste separately. Asbestos, some paints and solvents, fluorescent tubes, batteries, contaminated soil. Hazardous waste needs consignment notes rather than ordinary transfer notes, and stricter handling — never let it ride along in a general skip.
  5. Apply the waste hierarchy, and be able to say you did. Prevent, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose — in that order. It's a legal expectation as well as a tender question, and it's the honest answer to "what's your waste strategy?"
  6. Keep the records. Transfer notes for at least two years, hazardous waste consignment notes for at least three — but they also form part of the job's evidence trail, so file them with the job rather than in a drawer. See the retention table in Part 12.

Never invent or estimate a figure to fill a tender box — an unsupported carbon claim is a reputational risk, and the polite name for it is greenwashing.

Social value — the bit small firms can actually win on

Public buyers weight social value in their scoring, and larger main contractors mirror it in their own supply-chain questions. The current central-government model narrows the focus to good jobs and skills — real employment opportunities, fair pay and conditions, training and apprenticeships, and routes in for people facing barriers to work. It applies to central government procurements starting from 1 January 2027, on contracts from £1m, with a minimum weighting of 10% from £1m and 20% from £5m. Other public bodies can adopt it voluntarily, and many will.

Here's the thing worth knowing: a small local firm often has a genuinely better story here than a national contractor, and simply doesn't tell it. You employ local people. You take on apprentices. You use local merchants. That is social value — it just needs writing down with numbers attached.

Go deeper — where this connects to the ethics you already keep

It's the same subject as Part 11. Responsible sourcing, fair treatment of workers, modern slavery awareness and social value aren't separate disciplines — they're the CIPS ethical principles (Part 12) expressed as things a client can score. If you already ask a new subbie enough questions to be satisfied their people are properly employed and fairly paid, you're doing most of the work; the gap is usually just that nobody wrote it down.

Modern slavery, practically. A formal transparency statement is only required above £36 million turnover, so it almost certainly doesn't apply to your firm. But larger clients ask anyway, and their questionnaire won't care that you're under the threshold. A one-page policy setting out what you check when onboarding a subcontractor, plus the onboarding checklist you already run, is a proportionate and honest answer.

ISO 20400. The international guidance standard for sustainable procurement — guidance, not certification. Worth knowing the name for the same reason as the ISO standards in Part 12: so a questionnaire asking whether you're "aware of ISO 20400 principles" gets a confident answer rather than a blank.

Site Waste Management Plans. Legally mandatory in England until 2013, when the requirement was repealed — but many clients and main contractors still require one contractually, and larger projects routinely do. If you're asked for one it's a straightforward document: expected waste types and quantities, how each will be handled, and a record of what actually happened. Treat it as a job-level version of the waste discipline above.

Where the money actually is. Of everything in this part, waste reduction is the only item with an immediate, measurable financial return at your firm's size. Carbon and social value are commercial necessities that win work rather than save money directly. Sequence your effort accordingly: fix the waste and skip discipline first because it pays this quarter, and build the carbon and social-value evidence at the pace your tenders actually demand it.

Part 20

Negotiating, without being a shark

The self-assessment in Part 13 lists negotiation as the thing this playbook hadn't properly covered. This is that part. It is not about being tough — most of the money in a small firm's buying is won by being prepared, asking for things nobody thought to ask for, and being the customer a supplier actually wants to look after.

The mindset that works in a trade

You will use the same merchants and the same subbies for years. A win you extract by squeezing somebody gets taken back the first time you need a favour, a next-day delivery or a price held over a wet fortnight. Aim for terms you'd be comfortable telling them you'd got — that's not softness, it's the CIPS "use of power" principle in Part 12, and it's also just how repeat business works.

Preparation is most of it

Almost every negotiation that goes badly went badly before it started. Twenty minutes of preparation beats any clever tactic. Four questions, written down, before you pick up the phone:

  1. What do we actually spend with them? Pull twelve months of spend by supplier (Part 05). "We've put £68,000 through you this year" is a different conversation from "we use you a fair bit." If you don't know the number, you're negotiating blind and they aren't.
  2. What's my BATNA? Your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — what you'll do if this goes nowhere. A second merchant who'll genuinely match on the lines you buy most is real leverage; hoping they don't call your bluff is not. If your alternative is weak, the honest move is to improve it before the conversation, not to bluff in it.
  3. What do they want? Volume, predictability, prompt payment, a tidy account that doesn't generate queries, a customer their driver doesn't dread. Several of those you can give away cheaply — and they're worth real money to a merchant.
  4. What am I actually asking for, in order? Write your list before you start, ranked: what you must have, what you'd like, what you'd trade away. Going in with one number and no plan is how you end up accepting the first counter.

Price is the least interesting lever

New buyers ask for a discount. Experienced ones ask for the six other things that are easier for the supplier to say yes to and often worth more. Work down this list, by supplier type.

The two things you can trade that cost your firm almost nothing

Certainty and speed. A supplier will often price better for a committed volume over a period than for the same work drip-fed order by order — that's the call-off order in Part 13. And paying reliably on agreed terms is genuinely valuable to a small supplier, because most of their customers don't. "We pay on the 20th, every month, no messing" is a real bargaining chip. Just don't promise a payment rhythm the firm's cash flow can't hold (Part 06) — an unkept payment promise costs more goodwill than the discount was worth.

In the conversation itself

Move 1

Let them go first, where you can

The first number anchors everything after it. If they open, you learn where they are. If you must open, open with a specific, justified number — "£4,180 based on the three quotes I've got" beats a vague "can you do better".

Move 2

Ask, then be quiet

The most underused tactic in buying. Ask your question, stop talking, and let the silence do the work. Most people fill a pause by improving their own offer.

Move 3

Trade, never just concede

Every "yes" from you buys something. "I can commit the full year's timber if you hold that price to March." If you give ground for free, you've taught them that asking again is free too.

Move 4

Widen it when it sticks

Stuck on price? Add variables — volume, term, delivery, payment timing, returns. A deadlock on one number is usually a conversation that's too narrow, not a gap that can't be closed.

Move 5

Keep the "if" in front

Float proposals conditionally: "if we did X, could you do Y?" A conditional offer isn't a commitment, so you can explore without giving anything away.

Move 6

You may always pause

"Let me take that back and come to you tomorrow" is available at any point, costs nothing, and is exactly what you should say when you feel rushed. Pressure to decide now is itself information.

Opening an annual terms review with a merchant

"I've been pulling our spend together and we've put about £[X] through you over the last twelve months, mostly [categories]. We're happy with the service — I'd like to look at whether the terms still reflect that volume. Specifically: fixed pricing on our top lines, where we sit on delivery charges, and whether there's a rebate arrangement we should be on. What's possible?"

When their price has come in high

"That's come in above where I need to be — I've got comparable numbers around £[X]. I'd rather keep the work with you than move it, so tell me what you'd need from us to get closer: more volume, a longer commitment, a different delivery pattern?"

Pushing back on a mid-contract price rise

"I understand costs have moved. Before I take this to my manager, can you send me the increase in writing with the reason and the effective date, and confirm what's happening to the lines we've already got on order? We priced this job on the quote dated [date] — I need to understand what's genuinely changed rather than apply it across the board."

Closing it — and the bit everyone skips

A deal that isn't written down isn't a deal; it's a shared memory, and shared memories diverge exactly when money is involved. Close every negotiation the same way:

  1. Summarise out loud before you hang up. "So that's £X per unit, fixed to 31 March, delivery included over £500, 30-day terms — anything I've got wrong?" You'll catch a misunderstanding while it's still free to fix.
  2. Send the confirmation the same day. Short email, bullet points, what was agreed and from when. Use the agreed terms confirmation template.
  3. Get it onto the order. Agreed prices and terms belong on the PO and in the supplier record (supplier record), not only in your inbox. A rate nobody can find is a rate you'll quietly stop getting.
  4. Diary the review and the expiry. Fixed price to March means a reminder in February, not a surprise in April. Rebate deals need claiming — an unclaimed rebate is the most annoying kind of lost money because you already earned it.
  5. Tell your manager what you got, in money. "Fixed our top twenty timber lines to March, and got delivery free over £500 — worth roughly £X a month" is the sentence that makes your seat visibly pay for itself (Part 05).
Two lines you don't cross

Don't use a subbie's cash-flow trouble as leverage. Pushing a struggling subcontractor into a rate they can't survive on gets you a failed subbie mid-job, and it's the "use of power" abuse the CIPS Code exists to name (Part 12). And don't share one supplier's quote with another to beat them down. Telling supplier B their number needs to be under £X is fair game; forwarding supplier A's actual quotation is not — it's a breach of the confidentiality that makes competitive quoting work, and the trade is small enough that it gets back to people.

Go deeper — the frameworks behind these moves, and where negotiation isn't the answer

ZOPA — the Zone of Possible Agreement. The overlap between the most you'd pay and the least they'd accept. Deals stall not usually because there's no overlap, but because neither side has found it — which is why "what would you need from us?" is such a productive question. If there genuinely is no overlap, the right outcome is a polite no, not a bad deal.

Distributive vs integrative bargaining. Distributive is one fixed pie being split — a one-off purchase from a supplier you'll never use again. Integrative is growing the pie by trading things each side values differently, which is nearly every relationship you'll actually have. Small-firm buying is overwhelmingly the second; treating it as the first is the classic new-buyer error.

Where negotiation is the wrong tool. If a category is genuinely commoditised and you buy it constantly, a re-quote across three suppliers (Part 04) usually beats haggling with the incumbent. If a supplier is a genuine single source for something critical, hard bargaining raises your risk rather than lowering your cost — that's the "bottleneck" quadrant of the Kraljic thinking in Part 01, and the right play there is securing supply and finding a second source, not squeezing price.

Negotiating when you have almost no leverage. Small volume, one obvious supplier, no realistic alternative. You still have three things: how easy you are to deal with, how reliably you pay, and how much of your future spend you can credibly point at. Ask for non-price terms rather than discount, be honest that the volume is modest, and build the relationship for when your volume isn't. Pretending to have leverage you don't have is the one approach that reliably backfires.

And on nerves. If negotiating feels uncomfortable, that's normal and it fades with reps. Two things make it easier immediately: having your numbers written in front of you, and knowing that "let me come back to you" is always available. You are not required to be quick — you're required to be right.
