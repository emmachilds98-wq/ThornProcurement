Part 01

Procurement in a building firm

You know the theory. The thing worth locking in is how it actually lands on a trades job — where the money leaks, and what "good" looks like when your suppliers are merchants, plant-hire yards and subcontractors rather than a tidy corporate catalogue.

The whole job, boiled down, is turning a need on site into a paid-for delivery or a completed bit of work — the right stuff, right price, right place, right time, from someone reliable, with a paper trail finance and the taxman can trust. The recognised UK framework for this is the CIPS procurement cycle, and its north star is the Five Rights:

Right 1

Right quality

Fit for the job — the correct grade of timber, the right spec of aggregate, a competent subbie.

Right 2

Right quantity

Enough to keep the job moving, not a yard full of over-ordered stock that walks or warps.

Right 3

Right place

On the right site, where the gang can reach it — not the wrong depot 40 miles away.

Right 4

Right time

There when the trade needs it. Early ties up cash and space; late stops the job.

Right 5

Right price

What you priced the job at — not a number that quietly crept up between quote and invoice.

The point

They're linked

Miss one and it drags the others down — a cheap price that turns up late and wrong isn't a saving.

CIPS formalises the cycle into 13 stages, but for day-to-day it splits neatly into two halves — and knowing which half you're in saves a lot of head-scratching:

Strategic — Source-to-Contract

The thinking half: deciding what you need, finding and vetting suppliers, getting quotes, negotiating, agreeing terms. Done per category or per big package — e.g. setting up a groundworks subbie, or a year's timber deal with a merchant.

Operational — Procure-to-Pay (P2P)

The doing half: the daily loop of requisition → order → delivery to site → invoice → pay. High volume, lots of small orders. This is where you'll spend most days — and where small errors quietly stack up.

Together that's Source-to-Pay. Here's the full loop:

The one habit that saves you

Get the order agreed before the materials land or the subbie starts — never after. A PO raised up front protects the firm and gives you something to match the invoice against. An invoice that turns up with no PO behind it is where every overspend, dispute and awkward "did we actually agree this?" conversation comes from.

Part 02

Your patch & where the line is

Here's the trap when your manager isn't procurement-trained and the remit's a bit woolly: it's not that you'll do too little — it's that you'll quietly get blamed for things nobody actually gave you the authority to control. Sorting that is genuinely your first move, and it makes you look on-top-of-it, not needy.

You don't need a fancy job description. You need one page you and your manager both sign up to: what's mine, what's not mine, what I escalate.

Who does what — using a RACI (from scratch)

Not met RACI before? It's a dead-simple grid for pinning down who does what on each task, so nothing falls through the cracks and nobody treads on toes. Tasks run down the side, people across the top, and you put one letter in each box. The four letters:

  • R Responsible — the person who actually does the task (you can have more than one).
  • A Accountable — the one person who owns the outcome and carries the can if it goes wrong. Exactly one per row — that's the golden rule.
  • C Consulted — asked for input before it's done (a two-way conversation).
  • I Informed — just told after it's done (one-way, FYI).

Read each row like a sentence. Take "Issue the PO": you do it and own it (A R), and the site team is simply told it's done (I). That single line settles who raises POs — no more grey area.

Two things people trip on: there's only ever one A per task (if two people are accountable, nobody really is), and A and R are often the same person — that's the A R combo you'll see below, totally normal in a small firm.

Reality check for a tiny firm

You'll often hold two or three letters across a single row yourself — Responsible and Accountable for raising POs, and probably the "finance" column too (Part 06). That's fine — the point of the grid here isn't spreading work across a big team you don't have. It's making the manager's boxes explicit, so it's crystal clear what needs their nod versus what's simply yours to get on with.

Here's a starter grid — best filled in with your manager in a 1:1. Tweak every box, and you've turned a fuzzy remit into something you can both point at:

How to raise it with your manager

"Quick one — can we spend 20 minutes pinning down what I own, what I run past you, and how much I can commit without checking? I'll write it up as a single page so we've both got it to point back to. Just saves any crossed wires down the line."

This works because you're the one bringing order, and it's an easy "yes" for a non-specialist boss. You're not asking them to design procurement — just to confirm the boundaries. Frame it as protecting the firm, because it genuinely does.

Make your 1:1s count

Your regular 1:1 with the site manager is your main steering wheel — so treat it like a standing meeting with an agenda, not just a catch-up. Run the same five beats every time and you'll walk out with decisions instead of vague nods:

  1. Numbers — a quick spend / jobs update (the one-pager from Part 05).
  2. Decisions I need from you — anything over my limit, new suppliers, approvals to unblock.
  3. Watch-outs — jobs running over, late subbies, plant still on hire.
  4. One improvement — the quick win I'm working on right now (Part 10).
  5. Actions — who's doing what, by when — written down.

Keep a running note (a monday.com board or a simple shared doc) so nothing slips between meetings — and so you've a record of what was agreed.

Part 03

The buying engine

Your daily loop. Run it as a fixed sequence with two checkpoints — because the checkpoints are what keep the firm's money safe and keep you out of the firing line.

The three-way match, trade version

Before finance pays a merchant or subbie invoice, three things line up:

  • The PO — what we agreed to buy and the price.
  • The delivery note / receipt — proof it actually turned up on site (or the work got done).
  • The invoice — what they're charging.

Match? Pay it. Don't match — short delivery, price crept up, half the load's missing, damaged goods sent back? You've caught it before the money's gone. In a trade where merchant prices move and part-loads are normal, this one control saves serious money.

Watch out — signed delivery notes go walkabout

Deliveries get signed for in the mud and the note vanishes into a van door pocket forever. No signed note = no proof it arrived = you're paying blind. Make it a rule: every delivery note comes back to the office (a photo to a shared inbox is fine). It's the single biggest cause of "we paid for materials that never came" in small firms.

Watch out — segregation of duties

In a small firm the same person often requests, orders, receives and approves — that's the classic weak spot. You don't need a big team to fix it: just make sure no single person can both raise a purchase and confirm it was received/paid on their own. Even the manager casting an eye over delivery notes vs invoices breaks the risky loop. Since you're likely wearing the finance hat too, Part 06 has the realistic playbook for this when it's just you and your manager.

Give the site one front door

Decide how site asks you for stuff — one form, one channel, not five WhatsApps and a shout across the yard. Capture the same fields every time: what, which job, how much, by when, deliver where. Boring, but a clean intake is exactly what makes your job-costing reports write themselves later.

Running this in monday.com

The neatest way to build that front door is a monday form — site fills it in on a phone in twenty seconds, and you can make the job code a required field so it can't arrive incomplete. Each submission lands as an item you can then walk through to "Paid."

monday and Ariba divide up this workflow really well once each has a clear job — Part 07 is the full build: which system owns what, the boards to create, and the automations that do your chasing for you.

Part 04

Merchants, plant & subbies

Your three supplier worlds behave completely differently. Most of the messes you'll inherit trace back to sloppy records in one of them — and subbies come with legal strings (CIS, insurance) that materials don't.

Builders' merchants

  • Trade account & credit limit
  • Agreed pricing / discount
  • Any rebate deal (claim it!)
  • Payment terms in writing
  • Delivery vs collection rules

Plant & tool hire

  • Day / week rate clear
  • Delivery & collection charges
  • Damage / insurance terms
  • Off-hire process
  • Who can authorise hire

Subcontractors

  • CIS details (to finance)
  • Public liability insurance
  • Right to work
  • RAMS / CSCS where needed
  • Rate & retention terms
Money leak — off-hire discipline

Plant keeps charging until you formally off-hire it and get an off-hire number. A dumper that finished on Tuesday but wasn't off-hired till the following Monday is six days of dead money. Keep a live list of what's on hire, on which job, and chase the yard to collect the second it's done. This is one of the easiest wins you'll find in a trades firm.

Watch out — the bank-detail scam

A classic: an email that looks like it's from a merchant or subbie asking you to "update our bank details." Never change payment details off an email alone. Ring them back on a number you already hold — not one from the email — to confirm. This single habit prevents one of the most expensive mistakes in the whole function.

CIS & retention — know enough to not drop the ball

Usually your external accountant runs the mechanics (see Part 06) — but in a firm this small you may well be the one coordinating them, so know the shape well enough that nothing slips:

CIS (Construction Industry Scheme). When the firm pays most subcontractors, it generally has to verify them with HMRC and deduct tax before paying — commonly 20% (verified) or 30% (unverified), or 0% if they hold gross status. Your bit: never let a subbie get set up or paid without their CIS/UTR details captured and passed to finance.

Retention. On subcontract work it's normal to hold back a slice of each payment as security against defects — typically 5%, halving to 2.5% at practical completion, released after the defects period. Track what's held and owed, both ways, so money doesn't get quietly forgotten. Best practice per UK guidance: agree retention terms before signing, not when you're chasing it back later.

The above is general awareness, not tax or legal advice — the exact CIS mechanics and retention terms sit with your accountant and your contracts.

Getting quotes that actually compare

"Get three quotes" is standard advice, but it only works if you're comparing like with like — otherwise you're just picking the one that left the most out. The trick is to give every supplier the exact same brief, in writing.

Send all of this to each supplier, so the quotes come back genuinely comparable.
Tell themWhy it matters
Exact specGrade, size, finish, quantity. "Timber" gets you three different products at three different prices
Where & whenSite address, access limits, delivery date — a price without delivery isn't a price
What's includedDelivery, offloading, VAT, waste/returns — the usual hiding places for extra cost
Payment terms30 days vs upfront materially changes what the deal's worth to you
Quote deadlineA date by which you need it back, so it doesn't drift
How long it holdsMaterial prices move — ask how long the price is valid for

When they come back, compare total cost delivered, not the headline rate — then weigh in the things a number won't show you: can they actually hit the date, have they let you down before, and will they pick up the phone when something's wrong. Cheapest is not the same as best value, and you're allowed to say so when you explain your pick.

Keep the paper trail

Save the quotes you didn't pick, not just the one you did. It takes seconds, and it's what lets you show your manager why you chose who you chose — which is exactly the evidence that makes a savings claim believable at month-end. A quick note of "chose B: £40 more but could deliver Tuesday, A was 2 weeks out" is plenty.

Keep a tight preferred list

For the categories you buy again and again, agree a small set of preferred merchants and subbies with settled pricing. It cuts haggling on every order, makes cost predictable for quoting, and gives site an easy default — which is how you kill "maverick" buying (someone rocking up to a random merchant at full retail because it was closer).

This all rests on one thing

Every report above only works if every order is tagged to the right job at the point you raise it. Get that habit locked in — job code on every requisition and PO, no exceptions — and the reports basically build themselves. Skip it, and you'll spend the last day of every month guessing which job a timber delivery belonged to.

Set a rhythm and hold it

Predictability is the whole game. When your manager knows exactly what lands and when, the random "can you pull me a report?" pings drop right off. A sensible default:

On "weekly vs 4-weekly" — your manager's actual question

Switching a report from weekly to 4-weekly is a fair call, and mostly a cadence decision, not a technical one. Simple rule of thumb before you change anything:

Weekly for things that need action now — stuck orders, plant burning money on hire, exceptions. Monthly / 4-weekly for things you review and reflect on — spend by job, trends, savings. Match the frequency to how often someone will actually act on it, not how often Ariba can spit it out. (How to make that change in Ariba is in Part 09 — spoiler: it's usually a two-minute job.)

Presenting to a non-specialist boss

Lead with the answer, not the spreadsheet. One page: three or four numbers, a one-line "so what," and anything you need a decision on. Park the detailed tables in an appendix they can ignore. Translating procurement into plain "this job's £4k over, here's why" is a big chunk of your value — most people can't.

Part 06

When you're also finance

In a firm this small there's usually no one to "hand it to finance" — a lot of the time that's you too, with your site manager for backup and maybe one other pair of hands. That's genuinely fine: procurement and accounts payable are basically the same pipeline, and running it end to end is an advantage, not a burden. But two things shift — your controls and your daily admin.

You can't fully separate the jobs — so build "backstop" controls

The textbook rule is that different people should raise, approve and pay — so no one person can push a dodgy payment through unnoticed. When it's just you (± manager ± one), that's impossible. So you swap it for the next best thing: a second pair of eyes on the money-out steps, plus checks that catch problems after the event. This is exactly what auditors expect of a micro-business — nobody thinks you can be three people.

The handful of steps that genuinely need a second person — make your manager that person.
Risky stepYour second pair of eyesWhy it matters
Changing a supplier's bank detailsManager sees & confirmsThe #1 fraud target — verify by phone too
Adding a new supplier / subbieManager approvesStops fake or phantom suppliers
Authorising a payment runManager signs off the batchThis is real money leaving the firm
Any order to a friend / relativeManager approvesRemoves any conflict-of-interest doubt

In a tiny team, your paper trail is the control. Because you can't rely on separation, keep every PO, delivery note and approval religiously — the trail is what proves things were done straight if anyone ever asks.

Accounts payable — your other daily loop

This sits right on top of the buying flow you already know. When a bill comes in:

  1. Invoice arrives (from a merchant or subbie).
  2. Match it to the PO and the signed delivery note — your three-way match from Part 03.
  3. Code it — tag it to the right job, and to the right nominal code. (A nominal code is just the accounts system's label for what kind of cost it is — materials, plant hire, subcontractor, fuel. The job code says which job the money went on; the nominal code says what it was spent on. You need both.)
  4. Get it approved if it's over your limit.
  5. Schedule it into the next payment run.
  6. Pay & file — with the trail attached.
The 20 minutes that finds money — statement reconciliation

Once a month, take each main merchant's statement (their list of what you owe them) and check it against your records. It routinely turns up missing invoices, double-charges, and credits they owe you for returns. Suppliers make mistakes in their favour more often than yours — this is how you catch them.

Pay to terms — cash flow is king in construction

  • Batch it. Run payments on a regular day (say weekly), not ad-hoc every time an invoice lands — it's calmer and easier to control.
  • Pay on terms. Not so early you strangle the firm's cash, not so late you burn merchants and subbies. Fair, on-time subbie payment is reputation on site — and it's the spirit of the Construction Act and Prompt Payment Code.
  • Early-payment discounts are only worth taking if the discount beats the value of holding onto the cash.

A light month-end

You don't need a corporate close. A simple monthly tick-list keeps things clean and makes your reports trustworthy:

  • All invoices in and matched to POs
  • Account for GRNI — "goods received, not invoiced." If materials landed on site in March but the bill doesn't arrive till April, that cost still belongs to March. Flagging these stops a job looking cheaper than it really is
  • Supplier statements reconciled
  • CIS return done (with your accountant)
  • Retention schedule updated — held and owed
  • Spend-by-job reconciled for the monthly pack

Tip: this list makes a perfect recurring checklist board in monday.com — one item per row, rebuilt automatically each month, so you'll never wonder what you've missed. See Part 07.

Part 07

monday.com + Ariba, working together

You've got two systems, and that's actually a strength once you give each a clear job. The mistake almost everyone makes is treating them as rivals — or worse, typing everything into both. Here's the way to run them that gives you control without doubling your workload.

The principle: one is the record, one is the cockpit

System of record

SAP Ariba

Where the transaction officially lives: requisitions, POs, receipts, invoices, supplier master data, approvals, audit trail. If it's about money or compliance, Ariba is the truth. It's slow and heavy — but it's defensible, and that's the point.

Operating layer

monday.com

Where the work about the work lives: what's been asked for, what you're chasing, what's due, what's expiring, what needs your manager. It's fast, visual and flexible — brilliant at the things Ariba is bad at, like nagging people.

You're already using monday to simplify things for your manager, which is exactly right — that's the "cockpit" instinct. The upgrade is to use it for the whole operational layer, not just reporting. Here's how one order flows across both:

The one rule that keeps this sane

Never duplicate the transaction — only reference it. monday holds the PO number, not a re-typed copy of the order lines. The moment you start maintaining prices and quantities in both systems, you've created two sets of books that will drift apart, and you'll spend your life reconciling them.

If the two ever disagree about money, Ariba wins — every time. monday is the truth about status; Ariba is the truth about value.

The join between them: the PO number

One field does all the work. When you raise a PO in Ariba, paste the number back into the monday item. Now you can go from "the foreman's asking where his blocks are" to the exact order in Ariba in one click — and from an odd-looking invoice back to who asked for it and why. Make that column mandatory and never let an item move past "Ordered" without it.

Uses you might not have thought of

Delivery notes, solved

The "notes vanish into a van door" problem from Part 03 disappears if site photographs the signed note straight onto the order item in the monday mobile app. Proof of delivery, attached to the right order, before the van's left the gate.

Keep a private board of "how I did that in Ariba" notes. Rare monthly tasks stop being a re-learning exercise, and if you're ever off, someone can pick it up.

Cover when you're away

Because status lives in monday rather than your head, holiday stops being a crisis. Anyone can see what's outstanding and who's waiting on what — which also makes you easier to promote, not harder to replace.

Part 09

Changing Ariba safely

This is where a non-specialist manager and a big enterprise system rub up against each other. "Can you just switch that report to every four weeks?" sounds tiny — and sometimes it is. But changes in Ariba fall into three very different buckets, and treating them all the same is how small firms break their own setup.

The three buckets of "change"

TypeExamplesWho can do itRisk
1 · Self-serveReport schedule / frequency, filters, recipients, your own notifications, saved viewsYou, in the appLow — reversible
2 · ConfigurationApproval rules, spend thresholds, new fields, workflow steps, new report typesAdmin / SAP, via change requestMedium — affects everyone
3 · CustomisationIntegrations, custom-built reports / data schema, network setupSAP / a consultantHigh — specialist work
Your manager's example, decoded

"Change the weekly report to every four weeks" is almost certainly Bucket 1 — a scheduling setting on a report that already exists. Ariba lets you set report frequency (daily / weekly / monthly), recipients and retention when you schedule a report. You can most likely do it yourself in a couple of minutes: open the scheduled report, edit the recurrence, save. No change request needed. The real judgement call is the cadence one from Part 05, not the technical bit.

The trap is a Bucket-1 request that's secretly a Bucket-2 one: "…oh, and can you add a column for something we don't currently capture?" That's a new reportable field — it touches configuration and the reporting schema. Same casual sentence, totally different job. Your value is spotting the difference before you promise a timescale.

Which bucket am I in?

Why you can't just flip switches

In a big system, config changes ripple. An approval rule hits everyone's requisitions; a new field changes what reports can show; a workflow tweak can quietly break an integration. That's why proper changes go through a test environment first, then get deployed to the live ("production") site — standard SAP practice is build-and-test in a copy, then migrate deliberately, never edit live directly. You won't run that process, but you need to recognise when a request needs it, so you don't promise a five-minute fix that's really a two-week project.

Run a simple change register

No fancy software needed — one shared spreadsheet with these columns turns "who changed what?" into a clean, defensible trail:

DateAsked byWhat & whyBucketStatusNotes
25 AugManagerSpend report weekly → 4-weekly1DoneRescheduled, first run 1 Sep
25 AugManagerAdd "job code" column to report2AssessingNew field — needs admin; scoping
Pushing back — without saying "no"

"Yep, happy to sort that. Quick check first — are we retiming a report that already exists, or do we need new info we're not capturing yet? First one I'll do today; second means a proper change through the admin, so I'll scope it and come back with a timescale."

Never a flat no — it sorts the request into a bucket, sets expectations, and quietly teaches a non-specialist boss why some changes are quick and some aren't, without a lecture. Always ask "what problem are we actually solving?" first — nine times out of ten the real need has a simpler fix than the change they first pictured.

Part 10

Spotting & fixing what's broken

You're already in the seat, so forget a 90-day induction — the useful skill now is diagnosis: reading what's actually there, working out where it's leaking, knowing what "good" looks like for a firm this size, and taking that to your manager in a way that gets a yes. Do this well and you go from "the new buyer" to "the person who sorted our buying out."

First, map what's actually happening now

You can't fix a process you can't see — and in a small firm there often isn't a written one; it lives in people's heads and habits. So before you change anything, map the current reality. Quickest way: follow one real order end to end and note each hop exactly as it happens today, not how it's "meant" to.

  • When site needs something, how do they ask — text, call, shout across the yard, a monday item?
  • Who decides it's OK to buy — and is there any spend limit?
  • Who places the order, and does a PO actually exist — or does someone just ring the merchant?
  • On delivery, who signs, and where does the note end up?
  • When the invoice arrives, how's it checked before it's paid?
  • Where's any of it tracked — monday, a spreadsheet, Ariba, or nowhere?

Do that for two or three orders and the gaps jump straight out — that's your diagnosis, grounded in what really happens rather than what people say happens. Sketch it as a simple flow (boxes and arrows on paper is plenty) and you've also got something concrete to show your manager.

What "good" looks like — the target to aim at

This is the ideal shape for procurement in a small trades firm, drawn from CIPS-recognised practice. Use it as a checklist: where's your firm already there, and where's the gap?

The target operating model — and the gap you'll most often find in a small building firm.

No PO, no payEvery spend has a PO raised before it happensInvoices arriving with no order behind them

The professional backdrop — good to name-drop

Two things give your recommendations weight with a manager. The CIPS Code of Conduct is the UK standard for ethical procurement — fair competition, no conflicts of interest, treating suppliers straight. And in construction specifically, the Construction Act (payment notices, the right to adjudication, and no "pay-when-paid") plus the voluntary Prompt Payment Code set the bar for paying subbies and suppliers fairly and on time. You don't need to quote chapter and verse — just knowing this is the recognised standard means your "we should tighten this up" isn't just an opinion.

How to actually approach it — spot it, size it, sell it

Don't land on your manager's desk with a list of everything that's wrong — that reads as criticism and it's overwhelming. Work it in three moves:

Move 1

Spot it

Quietly observe for a few weeks. Use the target table and the diagnostic above to note gaps. Get the facts — a rough figure on the leak (e.g. "~£X/month in late off-hires").

Move 2

Size it

Rank the gaps by money saved ÷ effort. Pick one or two quick wins first — visible, low-risk, hard to argue with. Build the case in £ and cash flow, the things a builder feels.

Move 3

Sell it

Take one idea at a time, framed as a fix not a criticism. Show the before/after and what you need from them. Deliver the quick win, then propose the next. Trust compounds.

Framing it for your manager

"I've been getting under the skin of how we buy, and there's one quick win I'd start with — [e.g. plant that's sat off-job still on hire]. I reckon it's costing roughly [£X] a month, and the fix is small: [a live on-hire list]. Want me to run with that one, and I'll bring the next when it's bedded in?"

One idea, sized in money, framed as help, with a clear ask. That's how a non-specialist boss says yes — and how you earn the room to fix the bigger stuff later. You've got this.

Part 11

When things go wrong

Things will go sideways — that's the job, not a sign you're doing it badly. Here are the situations that come up most, and a calm way through each. None of these need you to be confrontational; most just need you to be the person with the paperwork.

The everyday sticky moments — what's actually happening, and your move.
SituationWhat to doWhy / watch out
Delivery is short, wrong or damaged Note it on the delivery note before signing, take photos, tell the supplier same day, ask for a credit note or replacement Once it's signed clean, it's much harder to argue. "Signed unexamined" is worth writing if the driver won't wait
Invoice is higher than the PO Don't pay the difference yet. Ask them to explain or re-issue. Only accept if there's a genuine agreed reason This is exactly what the three-way match is for (Part 03). Price creep is common and usually goes unchallenged
Invoice arrives with no PO Find out who ordered it and why. Raise a retrospective PO so the trail is complete, then have a gentle word about the process Don't just pay it quietly — that's how "no PO, no pay" dies. But don't make an enemy over the first one either
Supplier says you're on stop Check what's genuinely unpaid, sort the real ones fast, and tell them when payment's coming Being on stop can halt a job. Usually it's one disputed invoice nobody chased
Subbie wants paying, paperwork's missing Don't pay until the CIS and insurance details are in — but chase them today, don't just sit on it Paying without CIS details creates a real tax problem (Part 04). Be quick, not obstructive
Someone bypassed you and ordered direct Sort the immediate mess, then ask what made it easier to go around you — and fix that People go around a process that's slow or unclear. Usually a symptom, not defiance
Price rise mid-job Get it in writing with a reason, check the quote's validity date, flag the job-cost impact to your manager straight away Bad news early is manageable; bad news at month-end isn't
Your two default moves

When something's unfamiliar and you're not sure what to do, these two cover most of it:

1. Don't let money leave while it's disputed. Pausing a payment is reversible; paying wrongly is much harder to undo. It's completely normal to say "I'm holding this until we've checked it."

2. Put it in writing. A short email confirming what was agreed ("just to confirm — you'll credit the £180 for the damaged sheets") turns a fuzzy phone call into something you can point at later. This habit alone will save you repeatedly.

When you've made the mistake

You will, at some point — wrong quantity, missed a date, paid something twice. Everyone in this job has. The move is always the same: flag it early, say what happened, say what you're doing about it. A mistake surfaced quickly is an admin task; the same mistake found later by someone else is a trust problem. Managers remember how you handled it far more than what it was — and "I've spotted an error, here's my fix" reads as competence, not failure.

Handy phrases for awkward moments

Querying an invoice: "Hi — this one's come in at £X but our order was £Y. Could you check and send a corrected invoice or a credit note? Happy to pay the agreed amount in the meantime."

Chasing paperwork without nagging: "Just need your insurance and CIS details before I can get you set up for payment — send them over and I'll get you in the next run."

Someone wants it ordered right now: "Yep, I can get that moving — I just need it on a requisition with the job code so it's tracked. Two minutes and it's done."

Buying yourself thinking time: "Let me check that and come back to you today." — always available, never wrong.
